§ 01

The question

Markets react to headlines. That part isn't controversial. The useful question is narrower: if you score the tone of the news each day, can you trade on it tomorrow — and beat simply holding the index?

I tested it on the S&P 500 across 24 years — more than 6,000 trading days — in Python. Daily sentiment became the input; a daily position (in, out, or somewhere between) became the output.

Three models did the translating: LASSO, Ridge and Elastic Net. All three are linear regressions with a penalty for complexity. The benchmark was the most boring strategy available: buy the index and hold it.

The experiment, scrolled

Step 1 · Score the news

Every trading day gets a tone.

News is reduced to a daily sentiment reading — positive above the line, negative below. Underneath it, the index does what the index does.

Step 2 · Fit the models

In-sample, it looks like alpha.

Fit on the training years, all three models turn sentiment into positions that climb above buy-and-hold. This is the chart that makes people stop testing.

Step 3 · Hold data out

Draw the line it never saw past.

The real test: years the models were never fitted on. Everything left of the line is memory. Everything right of it is prediction.

Step 4 · Test

Out-of-sample, buy-and-hold wins.

On held-out data the edge disappears and the models fall behind the index. The in-sample gains were overfitting — the models had learned the noise.

§ 03

Three ways to say less

Penalised regression is the textbook defence against overfitting: charge the model for every coefficient it relies on, and it has to choose. The penalty strength, λ, sets the price. The three models differ in how they charge.

All three helped the models behave inside the sample. None of them could manufacture a signal that wasn't there. A penalty decides how much a model trusts its inputs; it can't make the inputs predictive.

In-sample is a story you tell yourself. Out-of-sample is the market's reply.

§ 04What I'd do nextNext steps, not done
  1. 01

    Walk-forward validation

    One split is one draw. Re-fitting on a rolling window and testing on the next slice, again and again, would show whether the failure is consistent or unlucky.

  2. 02

    Transaction costs

    Daily repositioning isn't free. Adding costs and slippage would raise the bar a signal has to clear — and make any in-sample edge look smaller still.

  3. 03

    Better sentiment, same scepticism

    Richer text models might read tone more accurately. Worth testing — under exactly the same held-out discipline, so a better input can't hide a worse habit.

  4. 04

    Regimes and multiple testing

    Check whether any signal lives only in specific market regimes, and adjust for how many variations get tried — the more you test, the easier it is to find luck.
