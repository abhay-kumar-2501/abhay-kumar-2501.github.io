§ 01The setupChapter one

Same experiment. Different participants.

Economics experiments usually run on people. Participants set prices, make offers and react to what they know — and to what they don’t. Over the summer my job was to rebuild one of those experiments with AI agents sitting in the participants’ seats.

I wrote the simulation in Python: four AI agents, a market, and the pricing and market rules that decide what each agent sees and how offers become a price. Not every participant knows the same things. That information asymmetry is the condition the agents’ decisions are evaluated under.

Then I ran it, analysed the results, took them to faculty, and revised the rules. Then I did it again.

§ 04What I learnedChapter four

Lessons from the rulebook.

  1. 01

    Fair rules are the hard part.

    Every pricing and market rule is a decision about what the market rewards. When a rule is loose, it shows up in the results before it shows up in the code.

  2. 02

    Replication forces precision.

    Human participants read instructions and fill gaps sensibly. A simulation can’t. Rebuilding the experiment meant writing down assumptions people never have to state: who sees what, when, and what counts as a valid offer.

  3. 03

    Feedback is a loop, not a checkpoint.

    Results went to faculty; revised rules came back. Over twenty-plus trials the design improved by iteration rather than by one good idea — which is slower, and more honest.

Putting AI agents into a human experiment doesn’t remove the assumptions. It makes you write every one of them down.
